💡 Design Thinking & Innovation

Learn to identify meaningful business problems, understand users, develop creative solutions, build simple prototypes and test whether an idea creates value.

← Back to Finance Hub Student Zone

This practical module connects design thinking with business management, finance, banking, entrepreneurship, digital services and sustainable innovation. Students learn by observing real needs and testing ideas—not simply by memorising definitions.

Learning outcomes: By completing this module, students should be able to (1) explain the design-thinking approach, (2) identify and frame a user-centred problem, (3) generate and select ideas, (4) prepare a low-cost prototype, and (5) collect feedback and improve a solution.

1. What is Design Thinking?

Meaning

Human-centred problem solving

Design thinking is an iterative approach that begins with understanding people and their needs, then explores and tests possible solutions.

Business value

Why organisations use it

It can help improve customer experience, reduce avoidable service problems, develop new products, simplify processes and discover new business opportunities.

Three lenses

Desirability · Feasibility · Viability

A useful solution should matter to users, be practical to deliver and make economic or operational sense.

2. The five stages of Design Thinking

STAGE 01

Empathise

Understand the people affected by the problem. Observe, interview and listen without assuming you already know the answer.

  • Interview customers or users
  • Observe their experience
  • Record needs, frustrations and workarounds
Finance example: Interview students about why they find monthly budgeting difficult.
STAGE 02

Define

Convert observations into a clear problem statement focused on a particular user and need.

  • Group common observations
  • Identify the root problem
  • Write a focused point-of-view statement
Example: “First-year students need a simple way to track daily spending because they often lose sight of small cash and digital payments.”
STAGE 03

Ideate

Generate many possible solutions before selecting one. Encourage different viewpoints and avoid judging ideas too early.

  • Brainstorm individually and in teams
  • Use mind maps or SCAMPER
  • Prioritise by impact and effort
Example ideas: Expense tracker sheet, WhatsApp reminder, weekly budget envelope or simple mobile dashboard.
STAGE 04

Prototype

Make a low-cost, early version of the chosen idea so users can react to something concrete.

  • Draw a screen or service flow
  • Create a paper mock-up
  • Build a simple spreadsheet
Example: Create a one-page Excel budget tracker with expense categories and a monthly summary chart.
STAGE 05

Test

Let intended users try the prototype. Observe what works, what confuses them and what should change.

  • Ask users to complete a task
  • Collect specific feedback
  • Improve and test again
Example: Ask five students to use the tracker for one week; note completion rate and usability feedback.

4. Mini case study: Improving a small business payment process

Illustrative classroom case

Problem

A small neighbourhood retailer receives cash, UPI and card payments. At closing time, the owner finds it difficult to match receipts with sales records.

Apply the five stages

  1. Empathise: Observe the closing process and interview the owner and cashier.
  2. Define: “The cashier needs a quick way to reconcile payment channels because manual checking takes time and can miss errors.”
  3. Ideate: Consider a daily reconciliation sheet, payment-wise summary or a simple point-of-sale report.
  4. Prototype: Create a spreadsheet with columns for transaction time, payment mode, amount, receipt reference and matched status.
  5. Test: Enter a small set of fictional transactions, ask a user to reconcile them and record errors or confusion.

Discussion: What information should be protected? Who can access the records? How would you measure whether the new process is better?

5. Tools students can use

Empathy map

Capture what a user says, thinks, does and feels. Base it on interviews or observation, not guesses.

Customer journey map

Show the steps a user takes, the touchpoints involved and where difficulties occur.

How Might We question

Reframe a problem as an opportunity. Example: “How might we help students understand and control small daily expenses?”

SCAMPER

Explore ideas by asking what could be Substituted, Combined, Adapted, Modified, Put to another use, Eliminated or Reversed.

Impact–effort matrix

Compare ideas by likely user impact and effort required. Treat the ranking as a discussion aid, not proof.

Prototype and feedback

Use paper sketches, slides, spreadsheets or clickable mock-ups to collect feedback before investing in a full solution.

6. Student practical activities

Activity 1 — Campus problem discovery (team task)

Identify one genuine, low-risk inconvenience experienced by students on campus. Interview at least five willing participants. Do not collect sensitive personal information.

  1. Summarise the common needs.
  2. Write one problem statement.
  3. Generate at least ten possible ideas.
  4. Select one idea and explain why.
Activity 2 — Finance service innovation

Choose a banking, insurance, payment, budgeting or investment-learning experience. Map the user journey and identify two points of friction. Propose and sketch an improved service.

Activity 3 — Prototype and test

Create a paper, spreadsheet or slide-based prototype. Ask three to five users to try it. Record their feedback and show at least two changes made after testing.

7. Assessment questions

  1. Define design thinking and explain its relevance to business organisations.
  2. Differentiate the Empathise and Define stages with a finance-related example.
  3. What is a How Might We question? Draft one for improving student financial literacy.
  4. Explain the role of prototyping and testing in reducing innovation risk.
  5. Apply all five stages to improve a customer experience in a bank or digital payment service.

Short practical submission: Submit a problem statement, user evidence summary, idea shortlist, prototype image and test-feedback table.
